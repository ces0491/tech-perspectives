## For *Running to Stand Still*

Leads on MD5, which sits in the post's second section. Correct code needing a
security patch anyway is the claim a reader is least likely to expect, and it
makes the case without the post's opening question.

---

For years MD5 was used to check that files hadn't been tampered with. Then in 2004 researchers broke it. Programs that used it for that job needed changing, however well they were written, and none of them had a bug.

That was where I ended up when I asked whether AI-written code could ever get good enough that software updates only add new features. Code can get very close to perfect. The world it runs in keeps changing, and some of those changes are forced on you.

What AI may change is who deals with them. They could become background maintenance that nobody decides on, like an immune response, and that makes the machinery doing the patching the thing attackers go after.

Built on work by people much closer to this than me, with their sources linked:

https://blog.sheetsolved.com/running-to-stand-still.html

## For *In the Long Run*

Leads on Crookes. A warning that looks wrong because it worked is the idea the
post turns on, and the story carries it for a reader who has never come across
the research.

---

In 1898 the chemist William Crookes warned that the world was heading for a wheat shortage, and said what would prevent it: find a way to make fertiliser from the nitrogen in the air. Within fifteen years chemists had done it. His warning reads as wrong today because people acted on it.

I've been thinking about that against the AI doom headlines. Research on emergency warnings and on fear in health messaging points the same way: a warning does more when it says how the harm would happen and what to do about it. The AI warnings I see in the headlines mostly leave out both.
